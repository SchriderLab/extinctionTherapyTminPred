# Additional analysis details for reproducibility

Companion to *Forecasting cancer treatment-switching windows from sparse longitudinal data*. The manuscript’s supplementary table “Clinical experiment inventory” lists the experiments, cohorts, targets and evaluation designs. The procedures below describe the initial simulation-based forecasts of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) rebound, the subsequent analyses using a common rebound target, and the simulation controls. TRAIN and TEST refer to the 184 VENICE training patients and 47 held-out VENICE patients in the later analyses. ABC denotes approximate Bayesian computation, GP a Gaussian process, MAE mean absolute error, and AUROC the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve.

<a name="original"></a>

## Historical early-PSA analysis and the common observed target

For the initial transfer experiment, we used four clinical measurements: baseline and the scheduled readings at cycles 1–3. We expressed each reading relative to baseline. Of 268 trajectories passing the global-minimum eligibility rule, 94 satisfied the original simulation library's early-input support rule and 90 also satisfied its forecasting-horizon rule. Direct ABC and its discrepancy-GP correction were evaluated on those same 90 patients. The constant comparator for direct ABC was the eligible simulation-reference median of cycle 5. Clinical Ridge used the 263 patients with an evaluable first-rebound target, patient-level cross-validation, and a training-fold median comparator. Repeated out-of-fold predictions were averaged within patient before calculating its reported metrics; patient bootstraps resampled these fixed predictions. This analysis preceded construction of the second reference library and the 184/47 VENICE training/test split. The original ABC summary vector was $[z_1,z_2,z_3,2b]$, where $z_j=\log_{10}(\mathrm{PSA}_j/\mathrm{PSA}_0)$ and $b$ was the least-squares slope across cycles 0–3. A clinical cycle corresponded to 2.25 simulation steps, with log-population interpolation at that grid. References required a first rebound-associated low at cycles 4–12, yielding 2,744 eligible references from 4,040 with complete early inputs. The prediction was the inverse-distance weighted mean of the 25 nearest targets. Support required the clinical 25th-neighbor distance to be no greater than the 95th percentile of reference leave-one-out 25th-neighbor distances (0.09268436); clinical targets also had to lie by cycle 12. These choices were clinically informed development settings, not an independent validation design.

For both the historical target reconstruction and the updated analyses, let $P_j$ be the positive PSA value at retained scheduled cycle $c_j$, with observations ordered by cycle. We define $T_{\mathrm{reb}}=c_j$ at the earliest index satisfying

$$
 c_j>3,\qquad P_j\leq P_{j-1},\qquad P_{j+1}>P_j.
$$

The comparisons use adjacent *available* observations; a missing scheduled cycle does not create an interpolated measurement. Equality is allowed on the declining side, but the confirming rise must be strict. This landmark is distinct from the first attainment of the global observed minimum, $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$, and from the analytic minimum of the fitted curve, $T_{\mathrm{fit}}$.

VENICE reconstruction selected randomized comparator patients using `RANDOM=Y` and `ARMCD=PLACEBO`. Positive `TMPSA` supplied baseline at cycle and day zero. Positive treatment-phase PSA laboratory records supplied later values; `ANLVISN` defined scheduled cycle and `LBDY2`, falling back to `LBDY`, supplied treatment-relative day. Within a patient and cycle, records were sorted by distance from day $21c$, then by recorded day, and the first was retained. Baseline plus cycles 1–3 were required. The historical eligibility gate additionally required the first attainment of the global minimum to occur after cycle 3 and to have a later observation. Requiring an evaluable $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$ then reproduced the 263 historical patient identities and targets. The earlier counts were 612 comparator patients, 506 with all four initial measurements, 482 with later PSA, and 268 passing the global-minimum gate. This global-minimum restriction defines the historical retrospective cohort.

## Complete-trajectory fits, the patient split, and the reference library

We described each completed trajectory using two exponential components (Stein et al., 2008, 2011). For PSA relative to baseline, $P(t)$, the model and its fitted nadir time are

$$
P(t)=(1-q)e^{-dt}+qe^{gt}, \qquad
T_{\mathrm{fit}}=\frac{\log\{(1-q)d/(qg)\}}{d+g}.
$$

Here $d$ and $g$ are the decline and growth rates, and $q$ is the initial weight of the growing component. We fitted the complete histories by bounded least squares on $\log_{10}$ relative PSA. Bounds were $q\in[10^{-8},0.95]$ and $d,g\in[10^{-5},3]$. We retained the solution with the smallest residual sum of squares across 120 starting points: $q_0\in\{10^{-5},10^{-4},10^{-3},0.01,0.05,0.2\}$, $d_0\in\{0.05,0.2,0.5,1\}$, and $g_0\in\{0.02,0.1,0.3,0.7,1.5\}$. Each least-squares run allowed at most 5,000 function evaluations. The original point-fit kernel was retained; the historical parameter bootstrap was not rerun.

A fit was excluded only when its analytic nadir was outside $[0,c_{\max}]$ *and* either $g\leq1.01\times10^{-5}$ or $q\leq1.01\times10^{-8}$. This joint rule excluded 32 of 263 fits and reproduced the historical 231-patient retained set. It did not require every retained curve to have an interior fitted nadir: 221 had $T_{\mathrm{fit}}$ inside the observed window, seven had negative $T_{\mathrm{fit}}$, and three had $T_{\mathrm{fit}}$ beyond follow-up. These cases remain in the parent cohort. Agreement between $T_{\mathrm{fit}}$ and the observed $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$ is reported both for the full parent cohort and for the in-window subset. Such agreement is retrospective curve description; neither a U-shaped fit nor its fitted $q$ establishes biological evolutionary rescue.

The sorted retained IDs were randomly permuted with seed 20261029; the first 47 were assigned to TEST and the remaining 184 to TRAIN. Clinical priors and simulator guidance used TRAIN only; prediction models and thresholds were fitted using TRAIN or its derived simulation reference library. MAINSAIL-trained models were separate controls. Because the parent selection and earlier analyses used completed histories, this is an internal held-out reanalysis of a retrospective cohort, not a claim of historically untouched external validation.

We generated 2,000 single-population simulations using the archived model and clinical generator. A TRAIN fitted decline rate was sampled from the empirical distribution trimmed to its 5th–95th percentiles. Turnover $r$ was sampled over $[0.05,0.40]$, and the Drug-1 sensitive-cell killing probability was set to $1-e^{-d}/\{2(1-r)\}$. Mutation rates were log-uniform on $[10^{-9},10^{-7}]$; target mean resistance effects and their log-scale standard deviations spanned $[0.20,0.90]$ and $[0.15,0.80]$, respectively. Standing-resistance fractions followed a log-normal distribution with median 0.035 and log-scale SD 1.20, clipped to $[0.0001,0.35]$, with an additional 10% probability of zero. Five pseudo-ages were sampled as integers from 15 through 23 and converted by the archived generator to one growth-equivalent age for the single population. The manifest used seed 20261030, five founders, a treatment horizon of 80 steps, a fourfold relapse stopping criterion, and up to ten additional seeds after pretreatment initialization failures. A viable initialization was accepted even if treatment subsequently caused extinction; no new biological parameter vector was drawn during these retries. One treatment step was treated as one nominal clinical cycle; this time mapping was not independently validated biologically.

Complete simulated trajectories with at least five positive observations were fitted with the same point-fit kernel, giving 1,998 fitted references. To guide their joint shape distribution, clinical and simulation fits were represented by $(d,\log_{10}q,g)$ and scaled using TRAIN medians and interquartile ranges. Each TRAIN patient allocated equal total mass to its 25 nearest simulated fits, with inverse-distance weights within those neighbors. The aggregated weights were mixed with 10% uniform mass across the reference bank. This procedure used the joint fitted distribution without interpreting $q$ as a literal resistant-cell fraction (supplementary figure “Fitted-parameter distributions in the training-guided simulation library”).

For the observation-variation sensitivity, we subtracted each TRAIN fitted log curve from its observed natural-log relative PSA, omitted baseline, and centered each patient's residual sequence by its median. Each simulated record received a residual sequence sampled in circular moving blocks of length two from one randomly chosen TRAIN patient's sequence; baseline remained unchanged. The residuals include curve-model discrepancy and are not a separately validated assay-error model. We extracted $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$ independently from the recorded clean and perturbed simulations, rather than assigning their analytic $T_{\mathrm{fit}}$ as the target. There were 1,649 clean and 1,827 perturbed references with evaluable observed targets; usable counts for a particular measurement budget also required all its input values. The primary analysis used joint-shape weights and perturbed observations; clean joint-weighted and clean unweighted references were specified sensitivities.

## Forecasting, tuning, clinical controls, and cross-trial evaluation

A budget of $n\in\{2,3,4,5,6\}$ measurements included baseline and scheduled cycles $1,\ldots,n-1$. The outcome was $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$ throughout. Models were trained using all complete TRAIN prefixes or eligible simulation prefixes, including training records whose event had already occurred by the last input. Evaluation separated all complete queries from the future-target subset with $T_{\mathrm{reb}}>n-1$. To compare budgets on identical patients, we also fixed membership to patients with complete cycles 0–5 and $T_{\mathrm{reb}}>5$: 28 of the 47 held-out VENICE patients and 94 of 181 MAINSAIL patients. Membership was not reselected separately for each predictor or input budget. These are future-event subsets of already selected eventual-rebound cohorts, not a screening population containing patients who never rebound.

ABC matched natural-log relative PSA levels after baseline and their first differences. Each log-level and first-difference coordinate was divided by its own reference-bank standard deviation; first differences were additionally multiplied by the slope weight. Euclidean distances selected the $K$ nearest references. For weighted ABC, neighbor weights were proportional to $w_k/(D_k+10^{-8})$, where $w_k$ is the reference's joint-shape weight and $D_k$ its distance from the query; rejection ABC weighted the selected neighbors equally. The primary exact forecast was the weighted mean of reference $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$, with a weighted-median sensitivity. Binary probability was the total normalized weight on $T_{\mathrm{reb}}>8$; the early class was $T_{\mathrm{reb}}\leq8$ in both trials.

Five-fold shuffled reference cross-validation selected $K\in\{10,25,50,100\}$ and slope weight in $\{0,0.5,1,2\}$ separately for each budget and reference variant. Reference feature scales were re-estimated within each fold. Exact settings minimized cross-validated MAE, whereas binary settings maximized AUROC. For each candidate, the classification threshold maximized balanced accuracy on its reference out-of-fold probabilities over their 101 empirical quantiles plus 0.01 and 0.99. Weighted and rejection ABC had independently selected settings and thresholds (supplementary figure “Weighted and rejection ABC for coarse first-rebound timing”). Neither clinical TEST nor MAINSAIL outcomes entered these choices. Constant comparators used either the eligible TRAIN median $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$ or the correspondingly weighted reference median, with the source explicitly identified; they were not recomputed from evaluation targets. An additional six-measurement sensitivity used the TRAIN median among patients with complete cycles 0–5 and no confirming rise observed through cycle 5. Of 182 training patients with complete inputs, 147 met this condition, giving a median $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$ of cycle 7, compared with cycle 6 among all 182. This rule used only rebound-confirmation status available at the prediction time. Both constants were evaluated on the same 28 VENICE and 94 MAINSAIL patients as the original comparisons; no model was refitted. Paired error differences used 2,000 patient-bootstrap samples with seed 202610292 and the training medians held fixed.

XGBoost controls used the perturbed joint-weighted references, the same observed $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$ labels, and log-level, first-difference and second-difference features. Absolute-error and squared-error objectives were compared with otherwise identical settings: 500 trees, maximum depth 4, learning rate 0.03, minimum child weight 5, row subsampling 0.85, column subsampling 0.90, $L_2$ penalty 1, no $L_1$ penalty, histogram tree construction, and seed 20260919. Joint reference weights supplied training weights. Thus the loss comparison did not substitute fitted-nadir labels for observed-rebound labels.

The updated discrepancy GP learned TRAIN residuals between observed $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$ and the primary ABC mean. Its standardized inputs were log levels and first differences, and its kernel was a constant-amplitude Matérn kernel with $\nu=1.5$ plus white noise; target normalization and two optimizer restarts were used. Clinical exact prediction used Ridge regression. A separate class-balanced logistic regression predicted $T_{\mathrm{reb}}>8$. Median imputation and standardization were fitted inside each cross-validation pipeline. Ridge selected its penalty by five-fold TRAIN MAE, and logistic regression selected inverse penalty $C$ by four-fold stratified TRAIN AUROC, using 13 log-spaced values from $10^{-3}$ to $10^3$; logistic predictions used threshold 0.5. The selected models were refitted on all available TRAIN prefixes. No clinical support filter was added to this new evaluation.

MAINSAIL reconstruction used positive PSA from visits named `Cycle k Day 1`; Cycle 1 Day 1 became cycle zero. Duplicate values were selected nearest the corresponding 21-day schedule relative to that patient's first Cycle-1 laboratory day. Values recorded with a leading `<` were parsed at the stated numeric value, reproducing the historical extraction. Baseline plus cycles 1–3 and at least two later observations were required, followed by the same local-low/rise rule. This recovered 181 patients with exact agreement to the archived identities and targets; no VENICE global-minimum or fitted-shape gate was imposed on MAINSAIL. Primary transfer applied the frozen VENICE-TRAIN models to MAINSAIL. Separately, MAINSAIL-trained Ridge/logistic models predicted held-out VENICE at four and six measurements, and a five-fold patient-level MAINSAIL analysis assessed internal prediction with tuning confined to each training fold. For the primary binary analysis, we retained cycle 8 from the exploratory VENICE analysis. With the sixth measurement at cycle 5 and the rebound still ahead, this choice defines a three-cycle early-rebound window. We repeated the six-measurement comparison at cutoffs of 6, 7 and 9 in the same patients (supplementary figure “Effect of the cutoff defining late rebound” and table “Six-measurement cutoff sensitivity in matched clinical cohorts”). At each cutoff we reselected the ABC matching settings by AUROC and the decision threshold by balanced accuracy using simulation-reference cross-validation. We tuned the logistic model in VENICE training data and classified probabilities of at least 0.5 as late. The cutoff defining the observed outcome and the probability threshold used for classification served different purposes.

Reference-size sensitivity sampled 250, 500 and 1,000 eligible primary references without replacement, with 20 subsets per size, and compared them with the full eligible bank (supplementary figure “Sensitivity to simulation-reference size”). The selected full-bank $K$, slope weights and binary settings were retained; distances were scaled within each sampled bank. Consequently $K/N_{\mathrm{ref}}$ changed with sample size. Paired MAE and AUROC intervals used 2,000 patient bootstrap samples with predictions fixed. These intervals condition on the fitted models and generated reference bank and do not include model-selection or simulation-generation uncertainty.

We also tested how the choice of PSA endpoint affected forecast accuracy (supplementary figure “Forecasts of first rebound and of the observed global PSA minimum”). For this sensitivity alone, we used the first visit attaining the global minimum of the complete recorded PSA history, $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$. The minimum had to occur after cycle 3 with at least one subsequent observation. The same rules were applied separately to the original and residual-adjusted simulated measurements. We excluded trajectories ending in extinction, whose terminal zero was absent from the positive log-scale records. This left 1,529 original and 1,497 residual-adjusted references from the same clinically guided library. ABC settings were reselected using these global labels in the reference simulations. Clinical predictors were fitted to the global target using VENICE training patients, and the empirical Bayes predictor located the global minimum in each sampled history. To compare the two endpoints on identical patients with six available measurements, we required both observed landmarks to occur after cycle 5. This gave 28 VENICE and 72 MAINSAIL test patients. Each model was scored against its own target and compared with the corresponding VENICE training median. The primary clinical target in the other analyses was $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$.

## Clinical empirical priors, early parameter information, and covariates

The empirical prior sampled 10,000 joint triples from the 184 TRAIN full-trajectory estimates, with independent Gaussian jitter of SD 0.08 on $\operatorname{logit}(q)$, $\log d$ and $\log g$. No additional fitted-nadir sign or in-window filter removed retained TRAIN patients. Early natural-log relative PSA, excluding the normalized baseline, updated the weights using a Student-$t$ likelihood with four degrees of freedom. Its scale was $1.4826$ times the median absolute deviation of the pooled centered TRAIN residuals, clipped to $[0.1,1]$; the fitted scale was 0.1562.

For each posterior draw, observed past values were held fixed and future values were generated from its biexponential curve plus the TRAIN residual-block process, at one nominal visit per cycle through cycle 80. The local-low/rise rule was then applied to the observed past plus simulated future. The posterior-predictive median, probability of $T_{\mathrm{reb}}>8$, and 2.5th–97.5th percentile interval were calculated after normalizing weights among draws with an evaluable event by the horizon. Probability mass without an evaluable event was saved separately; the interval is therefore conditional on an event by cycle 80. A separate latent-$T_{\mathrm{fit}}$ posterior median was retained only as a clearly labeled proxy, also scored against clinical $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$. The latent interval and the observed-rebound predictive interval are not interchangeable.

The parameter-dependence sensitivity shuffled $g$ among TRAIN fits within five rank-based $d$ bins before constructing the prior. It weakens the conditional $q$–$g$ association while preserving $d$–$g$ dependence only approximately. It is not a randomized intervention on resistance biology. A separate TRAIN-only information diagnostic excluded each patient from both its own parameter prior and residual-scale estimate, used up to 4,000 draws, and compared prior and posterior 95% widths for $\operatorname{logit}(q)$, $\log d$ and $\log g$ after four or six measurements. Full-history fits were reference estimates, not known biological truth; posterior concentration under this prior does not establish structural identifiability. Direct unregularized prefix fits were also attempted at five and six measurements, the minimum sizes supported by the original fitting routine, and their analytic nadirs were labeled as proxies.

At six measurements, the clinical covariate comparison used the 182 TRAIN patients with complete inputs. The feature arms were PSA levels/differences, baseline covariates, or their combination. Baseline variables were log absolute PSA, ECOG, BMI, log testosterone, hemoglobin, $\log(1+\mathrm{ALP})$, creatinine, phosphorus, calcium and albumin. Laboratory values used the median of records flagged `LBBLFL=Y`; columns required at least ten nonmissing TRAIN values. No additional audit of laboratory units or timing was performed. Models used the imputation, scaling and TRAIN-only logistic tuning described above and were evaluated on the same held-out patients.

To examine the effect of training-set size on early/late classification, we sampled stratified subsets of 45, 72, 100, 127 and 154 of the 182 VENICE training patients with six measurements. We drew 20 subsets at each size and fitted one additional model to all 182 patients. Each model was tuned within its own subset and then applied to both test cohorts: the same 28 held-out VENICE and 94 MAINSAIL patients. The bands show the central 95% of AUROCs across subsets of a given size. The separate baseline laboratory comparison used VENICE alone, where the selected laboratory variables had sufficient coverage (supplementary figures “Baseline variables and early PSA in clinical classification” and “Training-set size and early/late discrimination in both trials”).

## Sequential observed-rebound prediction and local-window classification

Sequential analysis retained the 184/47 VENICE split and applied the frozen TRAIN models to the 181 MAINSAIL patients. Updates began once four measurements including baseline were available and the current nominal cycle was at least 3. There was no separate four-decline entry criterion. At an update, only measurements available through that time were used; updates stopped after the first strict rise confirming $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$. Missing or nonincreasing calendar days stopped the affected patient's later updates without invalidating earlier usable prefixes. State filtering used actual elapsed days divided by 21, whereas the empirical trajectory predictor used observed scheduled-cycle times for the past and a nominal future schedule.

The state was natural-log PSA level, velocity and acceleration, with constant-acceleration transition dynamics and continuous white-jerk process covariance. A Student-$t$ robust Kalman update used four degrees of freedom (Kalman, 1960, and Roth et al., 2013). The observation scale was the median TRAIN Huber residual MAD, clipped to $[0.03,0.25]$. The jerk scale was $1.4826$ times the MAD of successive TRAIN curvature changes divided by the square root of elapsed cycles, clipped to $[0.005,0.15]$. The resulting scales were 0.05914 and 0.03757. A logistic classifier with TRAIN-standardized features, nonnegative coefficients and $L_2$ penalty 1 used $-|v_j|$, $\max(\Delta v_j,0)$ and $\max\{\min(\Delta v_j,\Delta v_{j-1}),0\}$, where velocity changes were divided by their actual elapsed intervals. The comparison model was logistic regression on standardized elapsed time since the fourth measurement, with $C=1$. Thresholds maximized balanced accuracy on the fitted TRAIN probabilities, not on held-out data or out-of-fold probabilities; the resulting state and time thresholds were 0.5498 and 0.4016.

Let $j_i^*$ denote the retained-measurement index of $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$. The window label was $|j-j_i^*|\leq1$. We separated a primary before-confirmation analysis, $j\leq j_i^*$, from a confirmation-inclusive sensitivity that also included $j_i^*+1$. The primary scope contained 228 updates from 47 held-out VENICE patients and 770 updates from 181 MAINSAIL patients; the inclusive scope contained 275 and 951 updates, respectively. The before-confirmation scope includes the minimum scan itself and is not restricted to useful advance warning. All parent patients eventually had an observed rebound by construction, so this experiment estimates conditional monitoring performance, not false-alert behavior among patients who never rebound.

AUROC and calibration were calculated over pooled held-out updates. Paired AUROC uncertainty resampled whole patients, retaining every selected update, 2,000 times with the models fixed. The model was fitted once on TRAIN; these are not five-fold out-of-fold results. Sequential exact forecasts used the same empirical posterior-predictive $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$ construction as above, updated at each available prefix, and were scored only when $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$ remained ahead of the current cycle. We reported pooled-update MAE, which weights updates equally, and the mean of patient-specific MAEs, which weights patients equally.

At each update we compared the empirical Bayes timing forecast with two estimates derived from VENICE training patients (supplementary figure “Sequential timing forecasts compared on the same visits” and table “Sequential timing errors on identical patient–visit pairs”). One always predicted cycle 6, the training median. The other predicted the median $T_{\mathrm{reb}}$ among training patients whose confirming rise occurred after the current nominal cycle. We compared each benchmark with empirical Bayes on the same patient–visit pairs. At one late VENICE visit, no training patient had an unconfirmed rebound; we omitted that visit from both sides of the comparison with the updated median. For paired intervals we resampled patients 2,000 times and kept all visits of each sampled patient together. Neither comparator used the test patient's future measurements.

## Resistance-history simulation diagnostic

In a separate simulation-only analysis, we tested whether early resistance-state information improved nadir prediction in 87 accepted trajectories from a clinically motivated stochastic simulation library. Each biological trajectory had one preassigned TRAIN-derived observation schedule; the target, $T_{\mathrm{obs,sim}}$, was its first observed global nadir after cycle 3, with a later strict increase required for inclusion. These 87 trajectories were selected from 1,024 attempts and were distinct from the clinical forecasting library. We replayed the same seeds with a resistance observer, requiring tick-by-tick equality of total population size before using the additional measurements. Predictors used population size and, where specified, resistance composition at observation cycles 0–3 (21 simulation updates per cycle). In addition to resistance-fraction and full-resistance ABC, we estimated effective sensitive decline and resistant growth by ordinary least squares on the positive early log counts and added these rate-based features to a hybrid ABC distance. The direct approximation was $\widehat{T}=3+\log(\widehat d S_3/(\widehat g R_3))/(\widehat d+\widehat g)$ when its inputs were valid; estimates were clipped to observation cycles 4–60, and undefined estimates used the outer-training median. These operational cell groups were not closed lineages because mutations could change group membership. The same stratified fivefold outer splits were used for all models, and ABC feature scaling and hyperparameter selection were confined to the training data, using an inner fivefold cross-validation. Paired intervals resampled the fixed out-of-fold predictions without refitting models. The direct $m_1$-based approximation was defined in 42 trajectories and undefined in 45, including 40 with no $m_1$ carriers at cycle 3.

## Observation-noise and recurrence controls

Each switch was scored using the extinction estimate for that patient and switch cycle in the saved landscape (cycle $t$ has stored index $t+1$). The first-increase analysis originally assigned a switch at cycle 12 if the four scans had shown no increase. This convention gave all patients a switch time within the same observation window. The 17 patients affected all showed growth between cycles 12 and 16. In these 17 patients, mean estimated extinction probability was 0.765 for a switch at cycle 12 and 0.576 for a switch at cycle 16. Across the full cohort, the corresponding estimates were 0.641 when switching was required by cycle 12 and 0.601 when patients waited for an observed increase. In the revised comparisons all switches require the rule's observed trigger.

For the noise experiment, we used the same drug 1 trajectories and the same post-switch extinction estimates at all noise levels. We retained the random-number stream from the original cycle 0–12 experiment and used independent additional streams for cycles 16 and 19 (seed 20260930). A count missing after the simulator reached its population cap remained missing on every noisy realization. Such a patient could still trigger at a scan for which a count was available. We report trigger fractions out of all 80 patients or all 8,000 patient–realization pairs, and extinction estimates among those that triggered. The supplementary figure “Detection and second-strike outcomes under noisy monitoring” reports both quantities as follow-up increased. The patient bootstrap holds the ABC predictor and the saved ten-replicate switch estimates fixed.
